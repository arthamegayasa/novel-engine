# Regression, time-loop, and foreknowledge stories: genre conventions

How the engine plans a book whose narrator knows the future, with *A Regressor's Tale of Cultivation* (ARToC) as the worked example. Chapter numbers are site numbers as used in `research/source-notes/`. This document extends `engine/structure/mystery-playbook.md` §5 (the Foreknowledge Ledger) and should be read with `engine/style/artoc-style-dna.md`. The craft report's notes on other regression works were outside the reading for this synthesis (only §5.3 was consulted), so the comparative claims below are limited to what §5.3 says about the readership and the market. Names and plot beats are examples, never material to reuse.

---

## 1. Three motives, and why "wanting to lose the cheat" worked

A regression story runs on one of three motives, and the motive decides what the loop costs.

1. **Redo.** The narrator returns to fix a wrong: a betrayal, a death, a lost war. The loop is a tool and the story ends when the wrong is fixed. Cost is low; the risk is that each loop is a cleaner draft of the last and the reader stops fearing anything.
2. **Accumulate.** The narrator returns to get stronger, richer, earlier. The loop is a grind and the story is the ladder. Cost is boredom; the risk is scale inflation and a protagonist no one can threaten.
3. **Escape.** The narrator wants the loop to stop. The loop is the antagonist, and every reset is a loss the reader can feel because the narrator feels it.

ARToC chose the third and stated it in the second cycle's first chapter: become strong enough to lose the ability and go home (ch 6). The craft report records this as the distinctive praise point for Korean readers (§5.3). It works for four reasons. Every reset erases allies and deeds, so sympathy comes from what each loop removes (ch 43: each regression's people are different people). The narrator's paradox, full effort in every life while wanting it to be the last, produces the thesis line about hearing the Way in the morning and dying content by evening (ch 12, 23, 463). The fixed-date death (ch 5, 24, 46, 49, 59) makes the loop a wall rather than a door. And the ending can refuse the reward: a story about wanting less can end with the hero throwing omnipotence away (ch 805), which the other motives cannot afford.

## 2. The cycle template and the cycle ledger

**A satisfying cycle contains**, in this order: a reset chapter that re-runs a fixed ruler and audits what carried over (ch 1, 6, 12, 17, 25, 35, 50, 60); a goal stated in one sentence (eliminate the regression, ch 6; the peak, ch 12; permission from heaven, ch 50); a recovery or re-learning montage compressed to a page (ch 7, 61, 224); a tour of who to visit first, since the cast has forgotten him (ch 63 to 67); one external project that is the cycle's distinctive shape (a ship, ch 68 to 74; a fruit, ch 86 to 91; a formation and a woman, ch 93 to 109); a catastrophe that arrives early or in the wrong tense (ch 75: a day early; ch 110: thirty years early); and a death that is also a breakthrough, phrased as an arrival (ch 16, 24, 34, 59, 74, 129). A cycle without a distinctive project is a replay; a cycle without a catastrophe that outruns his knowledge is a cheat.

**The cycle ledger** has four columns, and ARToC filled them in this order:

| Column | Rule | ARToC |
|---|---|---|
| **Resets** | body, position, allies' memories, geography | the rift lands him somewhere new every cycle (ch 3, 7, 13, 26, 36); the lover does not know him (ch 137) |
| **Carries** | memory and insight by default; everything else must be earned on the page and stated when it bites | consciousness size, with a bursting-skull cost (ch 50, 60); treasures bonded by marriage wine (ch 129 to 130); realm authority only after a late breakthrough (ch 431) |
| **Externalised save file** | one object that improves every loop and is handed to the next cast as a found thing, so progress is visible when the body resets | a manual the genius friend improves each life and the next friend receives as a found book (ch 5, 8, 19, 26, 37, 47); later a memory ledger fused to the heart (ch 169, 431) |
| **The persistent relationship or object** | one bond that accumulates despite the reset; without it, relationships restart and readers complain (craft-report §5.3, lesson 2) | the book found this late: a pendant tracked across nine cycles (ch 99 to 440); friends who ascend to a rank that keeps memory (ch 694, 718); portraits that rewrite the next cycle's comrades (ch 585 to 589) |

The last column is the one ARToC got wrong for its first 130 chapters and paid for in reviews. The engine fills it before chapter one.

**If the world does not reset** (only the narrator returns, or returns to a different point, or the world keeps what she did while she loses the body), the four columns still apply but are read from the world's side: what the world keeps of her, what she arrives knowing, what object or record the world holds between her lives, and which bond the world remembers even when she cannot. "Realm reached" in §5 and §9 then means "what she arrives knowing or able to do", and an "anchor move" means any change in where or when she returns.

Two further rules the book demonstrates. **Sentient attachments do not carry** (ch 132 to 133), so the regressor is alone by rule, and any exception is a plot event (half a soul dragged through at ch 699; a copy who diverged and does not return at ch 325). **Whatever carries can be taken**: the memory ledger is stolen so the hero cannot recall a place name (ch 307 to 316), and later it is revealed as the infection that trained the villain (ch 529).

## 3. The Foreknowledge Ledger rules, with examples

The playbook's rules: spend at least one known event per arc, invalidate at least one per arc, and make the narrator's certainty a flaw at least once per arc.

**Spend one per arc.** Herb prices, a cave, bandit treasure (ch 4, 18, 26); a palace's secret shelter (ch 32); a villain's castle coordinates (ch 112); how a madman's logic works, so he can be hired (ch 214); a prophecy's exact wording, fulfilled in letter with a universe raised inside a fruit (ch 500 to 503).

**Invalidate one per arc.** Timing is never stable: the abductor arrives a day early (ch 75), the massacre thirty years early because of a curse he did not know about (ch 110). He hears a boast in the wrong tense and the traitors he expected already own the continent (ch 90 to 124). His fate-reading tool is checked twice and is blind to the one class of actor that matters (ch 210). History itself changes: a mentor he banked on has been erased and a storybook has seven chapters instead of eight (ch 331). The resource itself decays (ch 159) and an entire cycle is missing (ch 222).

**Certainty as a flaw, once per arc.** He treats a changed person as unchanged: the lazy nobody has been goaded into a prodigy (ch 229 to 230); the woman in front of him is not the woman he loved, and his knowledge is harmful to the living one (ch 363). He clings to a stale plan and the people it was meant to save die anyway (ch 176). He narrates a flawless plan for three thousand words and is beaten in one paragraph by a consequence of his own virtue (ch 775). The structural version: 998 lives of being manipulated had one cause standing at his side, which foreknowledge could never detect (ch 497).

**Two additions the book makes.** The regressor's knowledge wins small tactical exchanges and loses every strategic one against a longer-lived mind; plan that antagonist. And knowledge is dangerous to say aloud: explaining would kill the listener (ch 233), so warnings sound like madness and the friend accuses him of choosing not to explain (ch 259); the mystery mechanic *is* the character conflict.

## 4. Moving the anchor

The regression point is the book's most expensive structural promise. ARToC moved it deliberately and each move did the same three things: it ended a mode of play the reader had learned, it made a body of past deeds permanent or impermanent, and it forced a new ledger.

- **Ch 140**: the point moves from the lower world to the upper one. One paragraph of relief that everything done below is now permanent; then a kidnapping in the same chapter. Consequence: a grave at ch 330 still matters; the old world becomes a place to return to rather than a save slot (ch 272 to 278).
- **Ch 281**: the death line is spoken, the reset does not come, and the point has fixed itself mid-void. The narrator is given a hypothesis to test (both fixes followed a visit to the old world) so the reader has something to hold.
- **Ch 326**: the rewind is a scene; he is conscious, chased, and spends a resurrection flower before landing on the familiar deck in the last ten lines.
- **Ch 411**: fifteen minutes. The gift of a quarter-hour saves nine fewer people than hoped.
- **Ch 428**: seconds before death; a one-minute do-over.
- **Ch 467**: at a prophecy. The safety net becomes a prison, and deaths 22 to 998 are logged as a list with one joke or one fragment of advice each (ch 468, 470).
- **Ch 543, 571, 674**: a condition is named without being stated; then a location; then the rule (the point fixes when the Future King observes an Ender's fate awakening beside the hero).

**How to plan it.** Decide before chapter one how many times the anchor will move and what each move makes permanent. Move it once decisively in the first third and spend a paragraph on what is now unrevisable; the permanence is the emotional payload and the rule is plumbing. Each later move is smaller in scope and larger in consequence (fifteen minutes, then seconds, then a prison). Give the narrator a hypothesis after each move and let one be wrong. Log every move as a ledger row with `what became permanent` filled in.

## 5. Death as breakthrough: the idiom and its risks

ARToC ends a cycle on the realm reached, not on the death. The death is phrased as an arrival: he gains the peak as he dies (ch 16), Three Flowers at decapitation (ch 24), the Five Energies standing (ch 34), a sphere as the lightning kills him (ch 74), a sword-carry at his own chosen death (ch 129). The reader closes the loop wanting to test the new power, which the next First Day immediately supplies (ch 0 to 60 notes, takeaway 10). The in-text ordinal-return formula is the cycle boundary, and it hardens over the book from passive (life ended) to active (he ascended), then becomes dark comedy when a return lasts three seconds (ch 675).

**Risks the book ran into.** The idiom trains the reader to expect a reset, so the promise had to be spent visibly: the death line spoken and the reset refused (ch 281), a resurrection that makes the cycle-end line a lie (ch 404 to 405), a regression forbidden outright (ch 649). Each spend is a one-time coin. Death can stop costing anything; the book answered by making the loop a prison (ch 467 to 471) and numbering the falls so elision carries the horror. The breakthrough can look free; the rule is humiliation before gain and a named cost per realm (`artoc-style-dna.md` §7). And once the reset is cheap, the arithmetic becomes the horror on purpose (ch 682).

**Rule for the engine.** Each cycle-end row in the ledger names the realm reached, the thing lost, and whether the ordinal formula is honest. A dishonest formula is allowed at most three times in a book and never twice in one movement.

## 6. Dramatic irony both ways, and the supporting cast

The standard gap runs reader-and-narrator above cast: the hero knows the four-day script, every colleague's fate, the disciples' deaths in another timeline (ch 0 to 60 notes). The book exploits it for comedy (tormenting a bully with knowledge he will never use, ch 2), pathos (training the children he killed, ch 26 to 27), and misdirection (the narrator's confidence makes the reader trust his readings, so his misjudgements land, ch 36 to 50).

The reverse gap is worked just as hard and is what the playbook means by "the cast knows what the narrator has forgotten about being a person." A colleague gets three chapters of POV so the regressor reads as frightening and hollow from outside (ch 156 to 158); another tells him his own origin story as hearsay (ch 163); a friend deduces the regression from genre literacy and says so (ch 663); the final antagonist watched the loops from the start and spared him for an oath (ch 685). By the finale the regressor is the last to know who knows, and the friends who have learned it use the knowledge to make him name his wish (ch 724).

**The reset's effect on supporting cast** is the genre's chief weakness (craft-report §5.3). The cast forgets him every cycle; the lover does not know him twice (ch 137, 363). Techniques the book used to re-introduce without boring the reader: one fixed trait and one variable, so the friend's speed to a realm is the thermometer of the hero's growth (ch 5 to 37); change the hero's altitude, not the character (ch 63 to 266); change who is looking (ch 156 to 158); withhold recognition on one side (ch 137, 266); introduce through a stranger's wrong summary and make the hero prove identity by fighting (ch 359 to 361), or through a bored newcomer (ch 751); a physical ritual encoding status (ch 612, 620); recast into roles that reopen wounds (ch 380 to 385); compulsions instead of exposition for amnesiacs (ch 465 to 467); hand the POV to someone new for a long stretch (ch 704 to 717). The structural fix: give at least one companion a mechanism to keep memory across resets and make reaching it a plot goal (ch 694, 718, 724). Readers forgave resets once bonds began to accumulate.

## 7. Scale management

ARToC runs from a street fight over herb prices (ch 4) to a Creator being gestated by the loop (ch 794). Where it kept character intact and where it lost readers:

**What worked.** Escalate by changing the *category* of threat, not the number: political, then cosmic. Alternate ceilings and floors: a hard cap stated (ch 700), the whole cast stripped to mortality for a century (ch 707 to 717), then an enemy class above the cap (ch 725). Keep the hero relatively weaker as absolute power grows: after beating a rival he is split in half by the next being's first strike (ch 361, 370). Give geological spans a mortal face (ch 581 to 583). De-escalate before the end: the final duel is fought in mortal bodies in the town where life one began (ch 777 to 781), and the epilogue's first problem is a tabloid headline (ch 810).

**The milestone-not-calendar rule.** Time is measured in what changed, never in years alone: years per star (ch 44 to 45), bones crumbled per decade (ch 393), footsteps as millennia (ch 786). Readers accept a skip of twenty years in a sentence when the *result* is dramatised (craft-report §5.3, lesson 5). Numbers beyond intuition are counted as deaths, breaths, or footsteps.

**Where ARToC lost readers.** Tens of thousands of years flattened character (§5.3); trillions in single sentences (ch 418, 426); a 190,000-year solitude (ch 404 to 410); loop arithmetic the translator had to correct (ch 682); a disliked late arc that likely corresponds to the longest cycle's realm sojourn (ch 356 to 410; an inference from the romanisation). Readers quit around chapters 200 to 400, where cycles run 59, 45, and 85 chapters without a reset. The engine caps a cycle near forty and puts a realm arrival where a First Day would have fallen.

## 8. Ending a regression story

**What the notes say ARToC did.** The loop was never a curse to be cured; it was a body being built. The Audience Chamber is where the contract was signed (ch 788); Enders are manufactured in batches (ch 790, 813); the servant who rode the hero's shoulder from cycle 10 is the final antagonist (ch 788); the hero's own loops gestate a Creator (ch 776, 794). The torn-out cycle was not amnesia but a payment made later in time than the forgetting (ch 806). The regression was severed when the servant took the mantra (ch 781) and the reward was refused: Omnipotence thrown into another world (ch 805). The final image is a requiem for a green deer (ch 805), then, in the side stories, a former enemy carried home by the hero's true body (quoted, ch 861: "Thus, the story on Earth concludes."). The afterword names the theme as gratitude for life (ch 808), and the hero's rebuttal to omniscience is a ledger of trivial gratitude: soy sauce, potatoes, more hair than his friend (ch 804a, 806).

**What the notes leave unclear.** Why he regresses has three partial answers (a god's mantra wearing a man's ego, ch 651; a soul the mantra clings to, ch 652; the gestation, ch 794) and no recorded sentence unifies them. Whether the severing at ch 781 and the refusal at ch 805 are one mechanism is not stated. The afterword admits late decisions (ch 808).

**What an original book must decide before chapter one.**

1. **Why does he regress?** The answer must be a filled ledger row (mystery-playbook §1, rule 1) and the hidden hand must be on stage from the first ten percent (rule 3). ARToC's hidden hand was named at ch 191 and rode the hero's shoulder as a servant; the first ten percent should contain its equivalent.
2. **What ends it?** Choose between the loop being severed by an agent (ch 781), refused by the hero (ch 805), or completed by its own purpose (ch 794), and decide whether the ending is the same scene as the last breakthrough. ARToC put the severing and the refusal twenty-four chapters apart and the notes cannot say whether they are one thing; decide.
3. **What is the final image**, and is it the hero's? ARToC's is not: a requiem, then a former enemy choosing attachment over omnipotence (ch 861). The thesis restated in a minor key by someone else is the book's last move.

## 9. Checklist for `/novel-new` when the genre is regression

1. **Motive declared**: redo, accumulate, or escape, in one sentence in the bible; if escape, the goal sentence appears in the first reset chapter (ch 6).
2. **Why he regresses**: a ledger row with the truth field filled; the hidden hand has an on-stage role in the first ten percent.
3. **What ends it**: written, with the chapter band and whether it coincides with the last breakthrough.
4. **The fixed ruler**: two First Day beats the reader sees unchanged on at least half of the planned resets (minimum three) before the first break (ARToC: eight unchanged, ch 1 to 60).
5. **The cycle ledger's four columns** filled; the persistent relationship or object exists from chapter one, or its absence is itself the plot and the first carry-over arrives by the end of arc 1. If the world does not reset, read the columns from the world's side (§2).
6. **Carry-over rules** scheduled with the chapter where each first costs something (ch 60, 133).
7. **Anchor moves** planned: how many, where, what each makes permanent; one decisive move in the first third.
8. **Cycle lengths** capped near forty; longer cycles get a realm arrival or dream world running the template without a reset (ch 139, 339, 380).
9. **The Foreknowledge Ledger** seeded with one spend, one invalidation, and one certainty-as-flaw per arc, plus one antagonist who has lived longer than the narrator.
10. **The fixed-date death** or its equivalent: one constraint the loop cannot bypass, stated dryly once per cycle before it is explained (ch 5, 7, 16, 23, 46).
11. **Cycle-end rows** naming the realm reached, the thing lost, and whether the ordinal formula is honest; at most three dishonest formulas per book.
12. **Reverse irony scheduled**: one witness POV per movement (ch 156 to 158) and one companion who learns the secret by a route the hero did not choose (ch 663, 694, 718).
13. **Re-introduction method** assigned per recurring character per cycle from §6; no re-entry by recap.
14. **Scale plan**: category of threat per movement; a mortal-scale vignette inside every span beyond intuition; a de-escalation before the finale.
15. **The final image** decided, and whether it belongs to the hero.
