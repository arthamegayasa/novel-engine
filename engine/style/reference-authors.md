# Reference-author panel

The crosscheck does not ask "is this good?" It asks sixteen narrow, checkable questions, each tied to a named writer's documented principle. The reviewer applies the principle; it does not imitate the prose. Panels are chosen per book in bible §8 (three to five lenses). Different lenses reveal different tradeoffs: the ruling lens depends on the chapter type.

Each entry: the lens, the checkable question, the documented rule it rests on, and what the lens tends to get wrong if over-applied. Sources are summarised in `research/craft-report.md` §2 and §6.2.

## Prose lenses

### Ursula K. Le Guin: sound, rhythm, crowding and leaping
- **Ask:** Read three paragraphs aloud. Where does the rhythm flatten? Which transition could be a leap (a clause instead of a scene)? Which passage deserves crowding (slowed to its grain, loaded with specific detail)?
- **Rule:** *Steering the Craft*: story is "something moving, something happening, something or somebody changing"; conflict is not the only engine (relating, losing, discovering also move a story); "crowding and leaping" as the two basic moves of pacing.
- **Over-applied:** everything becomes lyrical. Keep the dry passages dry.

### Chuck Palahniuk: thought verbs and thesis openers
- **Ask:** List every thought verb (knew, realised, wanted, remembered, wondered, believed, felt) and every paragraph that opens with a conclusion. For each, what concrete detail would make the reader draw the conclusion themselves?
- **Rule:** "Thought Verbs" essay: thinking is abstract; make the case like a lawyer, detail by detail; do not leave characters alone (they drift into thought); do not use "remember" as a bridge to backstory.
- **Over-applied:** characters become opaque strangers. Budget six thought verbs per 1,000 words rather than zero.

### Ernest Hemingway: omission
- **Ask:** What does the chapter state that the ledger shows the reader could infer? Cut it. What does the chapter omit that the ledger does *not* know? That is a hollow, not an iceberg; fill the ledger or the chapter.
- **Rule:** the iceberg: a writer who knows may omit; omission of what the writer does not know leaves holes.
- **Over-applied:** cryptic. Omission needs a submerged mass.

### John Gardner: psychic distance
- **Ask:** Tag each paragraph 1 (reportorial, "It was winter in the sect, 1143") to 5 (sensory fragments, no narrator). Flag jumps of more than two levels inside a paragraph. Does the chapter open at 2 to 3 and reach 4 to 5 at the turn?
- **Rule:** *The Art of Fiction*: the "vivid and continuous dream" breaks when distance lurches.
- **Over-applied:** monotone closeness. Distance is a dial, not a destination.

### Stephen King: puff
- **Ask:** Which ten percent goes? List adverbs. List sentences that say the same thing as the one before.
- **Rule:** "Second draft = first draft minus ten percent"; "the road to hell is paved with adverbs."
- **Over-applied:** stripped of texture. Cut puff, not idiosyncrasy.

### Francine Prose: close reading
- **Ask:** Pick the chapter's best sentence and its worst. Why is each what it is, at the level of word choice?
- **Rule:** *Reading Like a Writer*: attention at the level of words, then sentences, then paragraphs; rhythm from alternating long and short.
- **Over-applied:** paralysis. One best, one worst, move on.

## Scene lenses

### Dwight Swain and Jim Butcher: scene integrity
- **Ask:** Name the point-of-view character, their goal in the scene, the conflict (between characters, not just weather), and the setback. Classify the outcome: Yes / Yes-but / No / No-and-furthermore. If there is a sequel (emotion, reasoning, anticipation, choice), is it in that order? Did any reaction arrive before its stimulus?
- **Rule:** Swain's motivation-reaction units; Butcher's LiveJournal essays: "conflict happens between characters"; "pick the person with the most at stake"; "No, and furthermore" is the fix for a slowing plot.
- **Over-applied:** every scene becomes a fight. Setbacks can be quiet.

### Shawn Coyne (Story Grid): five commandments and the value shift
- **Ask:** Identify the inciting incident, the progressive complication, the turning point, the crisis (name both options: best bad choice, or irreconcilable goods), the climax (the action taken), the resolution. Which value shifted and in which direction? Is the direction the same as the last two chapters?
- **Rule:** the five commandments apply fractally; a scene that shifts no value is dead.
- **Over-applied:** melodrama in every scene. The shift can be small; it must be real.

### Brandon Sanderson: promise, progress, payoff; laws of magic
- **Ask:** Which promise did this chapter progress, and how would a reader notice? Did any power or revelation resolve a conflict the reader could not have anticipated? Was the cost of the power shown?
- **Rule:** lecture notes: readers drop books when there are not enough signposts of progress; First Law (resolving conflict with magic is proportional to reader understanding); Second Law (limits beat powers); Third Law (expand before adding).
- **Over-applied:** mechanical signposting. Progress can be emotional.

### George Saunders: escalation and causality
- **Ask:** Did this unit both entertain and advance? What caused what (the king died *of grief*)? Where did the writer hoard a detail that should have been spent?
- **Rule:** *A Swim in a Pond in the Rain*: "always be escalating"; "having made something happen, make it matter"; refuse the first version.
- **Over-applied:** breathless. Rest chapters escalate quietly.

### Donald Maass: emotional mode
- **Ask:** For each emotional beat: inner mode (told), outer mode (shown through action and subtext), or other mode (the reader feels what the character does not)? If inner, is it the first, obvious feeling? At the most painful beat, did the prose pull back?
- **Rule:** *The Emotional Craft of Fiction*: dig past the stock first feeling; "when emotions are highly painful, pull back."
- **Over-applied:** coldness. Pull back at the peak, not everywhere.

### Lisa Cron and Robert McKee: misbelief and the gap
- **Ask:** What does the protagonist believe that is wrong, and how did this chapter test it? Where is the gap between what the character expected when acting and what happened? A scene where they get exactly what they expected is a scene not worth writing.
- **Rule:** *Wired for Story*: the misbelief is the spine; *Story*: the gap is the unit of drama.
- **Over-applied:** every expectation foiled. Sometimes a plan works and that is the trap.

## Mystery lenses

### Agatha Christie: fair play and misdirection
- **Ask:** Is every clue a true fact? Is the misdirection a wrong inference rather than a false fact? Was the plant followed by a louder beat? Is the hidden hand already on stage under some identity? Can you "saw the clue in half" and place the halves far apart?
- **Rule:** Hope and Hope's catalogue of thirty-three techniques (plenty of nothing, identity theft, sawing the clue in half, clue clusters); Knox and Van Dine fair-play rules; plain prose as camouflage.
- **Over-applied:** puzzle-box coldness. Clues live in scenes about something else.

### Gene Wolfe: fair unreliability
- **Ask:** Does the narrator state anything false that the text never exposes? Are omissions and misreadings logged in the ledger as `misread`? Does the narrator order events to his own ends, and does the text let an attentive reader notice?
- **Rule:** Severian misleads by omission and ambiguity, not by lies; clues are given once; rereading shows what was already there.
- **Over-applied:** obscurity. One unreliable layer at a time.

## Serial lenses

### Terry Pratchett (comic lens): undercutting from inside character
- **Ask:** Is there a tense beat that wants a human, deflating detail? Does the joke come from a character's logic (what they would notice, what they would get wrong) rather than from the narrator winking? Does the comic beat sit *beside* the turn, not inside it?
- **Rule:** genre practice: the funniest line is the one a character means seriously.
- **Over-applied:** nothing lands because everything is undercut. One comic beat per tense chapter.

### The Korean serial reader: debt and repayment (goguma / sida)
- **Ask:** Where is the frustration debt (the humiliation, the stall, the loss) and when is it repaid? Is the repayment earned on-page, or handed over? Does the chapter end on a pull that the next chapter honours rather than cheats? Has the antagonist won an exchange before losing?
- **Rule:** platform norms: cider repays sweet potato; face-slapping reads cheap when the antagonist is humiliated in the chapter he is introduced; hard cliffhangers resolved by a cheat in the next first paragraph teach readers not to trust them.
- **Over-applied:** pandering. Some debts stay unpaid for a long time, on purpose, and are logged.

## Choosing a panel

| Chapter type | Ruling lenses | Supporting lenses |
|---|---|---|
| Reveal / payoff | Christie, Wolfe | Coyne, Hemingway |
| Training / progression | Butcher, Sanderson | Saunders, Korean reader |
| Rest / slice of life | Le Guin, Pratchett | Maass, Prose |
| Grief / loss | Maass, Hemingway | Gardner, Palahniuk |
| Argument / politics | Butcher, Cron-McKee | Korean reader, Christie |
| Cycle reset / first day | Sanderson, Coyne | Le Guin (leaping), Saunders |
| Climax | Butcher (climax anatomy), Coyne | Sanderson, King |

Each lens returns a verdict (pass / revise), any necessary concrete edits with quoted lines, and something to keep. Apply the distinct principle; allow agreement where warranted and never invent disagreements or edits to fill a quota.
