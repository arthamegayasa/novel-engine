# ARToC style DNA: the portable version

Operational rules for the writer and the reviewer when a project runs in regression-cultivation mode. Each move gives the rule, one ARToC example with a chapter number (site numbering), and how to use it in an original book. This document specialises `engine/style/voice-guide.md`; where a move narrows a house rule, the house rule is named. Where a move would loosen one, the tension is stated and the project bible §2 must declare the mode explicitly. The full evidence is in `research/artoc-analysis.md`. Names and plot beats are examples, never material to reuse.

---

## 1. Voice moves

1. **One clause per paragraph as default; a full paragraph is an event.** ARToC: thirty one-line paragraphs, then three sentences of exposition the reader feels as a slow-down (ch 61 to 130 notes). Original: this inverts voice-guide rule 15 (the one-liner as the once-per-chapter hammer). In this mode the hammer is the three-sentence paragraph; the bible must say so, and the reviewer reads rule 15 inverted. Rules 6 and 7 (length and shape variety) still apply inside the chapter.

2. **Interior calculation in single quotes; conviction as a bare statement.** ARToC: a one-word thought of disbelief, then a tagless line stating what he will do (ch 267). Original: never tag the bare line; its lack of a tag is what makes it conviction.

3. **Four speech channels, kept distinct for the whole book.** ARToC: quotes, single quotes, brackets for speech that bypasses the mouth (ch 2, 42), a colon fence for divine will (ch 338). Original: choose your own marks, define them in the bible, and never let a channel drift; the reader should know a speaker's rank before the words.

4. **Onomatopoeia as its own line to stage pace.** ARToC: a sound, then a one-line reaction; fights as a column of sounds with captions (ch 13, 265). Original: in English, use a short noun or verb fragment rather than romanised sound (a crack; the door; glass); never describe the blow the sound already made.

5. **Countdown lists as rhythm engine.** ARToC: steps toward a fox (ch 25), rituals performed (ch 51), thrusts counted to 80 quadrillion (ch 692). Original: a count does the work of a montage and of an emotion; let the number be exact (voice-guide rule 34).

6. **Numbers substitute for montage.** ARToC: 2,054 duels, nine wins, six losses (ch 86). Original: a tally the character would keep is a scene; a tally the narrator invents is padding.

7. **Repetition as rhythm, not as triplet.** ARToC: a verb three times with rising punctuation (ch 148, 412); a question five times as a heart demon (ch 729). Original: voice-guide rule 14 bans list triplets; repetition of one short sentence across a chapter with shifting meaning is the sanctioned form (voice-guide §8).

8. **Mantra introduced as joke, hardened into policy, paid at death, redefined by a rival.** ARToC: madness as the talentless man's method (ch 14), literalised (ch 51 to 53), made a theory (ch 241), made a prison (ch 256). Original: pick two mantras per book and schedule their drift in the ledger; a mantra that never changes meaning is a slogan.

9. **The narrator corrects everyone who calls him a genius.** ARToC: ch 13, 37; he asks not to be called senior or esteemed and is ignored (ch 412 to 470 notes). Original: the correction is characterisation; let it fail every time.

10. **Someone else names his cost.** ARToC: the friend blurts that the white hair is two hundred spirits (ch 31). Original: the narrator may hide his sacrifice; a third party exposes it, and the narrator's only reply is to ask them to stop.

11. **Move names printed as lines, liturgically; the enemy's moves as single words, the hero's as long phrases.** ARToC: ch 13, 538; in the finale the villain's moves are one syllable each and the hero's are mountain phrases (ch 770 to 817 notes). Original: typography encodes who is complete and who is accumulating.

12. **The self-insult that becomes a banner.** ARToC: insect, from the enemy's mouth (ch 21) to his own (ch 34, 352). Original: give the narrator one slur to reclaim; track it like a clue.

13. **Rhetorical self-questioning before a turn, inside the cap.** ARToC: why, then why, before most realisations (ch 331, 353, 355). Original: voice-guide rule 25 allows this only if the bible declares it and caps it at three per thousand words; the baseline runs about three, so the cap is the mode's natural rate. Render the realisation itself as a thing seen differently, not a verb of knowing (rule 19).

14. **The narrator leaves the page when he is no longer the strongest piece.** ARToC: cutaways go from one per arc to one chapter in three (ch 544 to 609); seven chapters without "I", then he returns in six lines (ch 686 to 692). Original: let the camera's absence be the stakes.

15. **A liar whose inverse is reliable.** ARToC: read him inverted, named as a rule (ch 485), then he announces the one day he speaks plainly (ch 494). Original: a speech rule is an idiolect (voice-guide rule 27) that doubles as a mystery device.

16. **Earth vocabulary that decays.** ARToC: an archaic word for coffee, corrected by a colleague (ch 659). Original: the narrator's comparison domain (voice-guide rule 36) erodes measurably across the book; log the erosion.

## 2. Humour moves

1. **Undercut after grandeur, one line.** ARToC: the house is vaporised; one line of mourning; then analysis of the debris (ch 16). Original: the undercut is a single clause, never a paragraph.

2. **Register clash as the base note.** ARToC: a cult that runs a forty-hour week with wages (ch 342 to 344); a god's job interview (ch 831). Original: voice-guide rule 32; the clash comes from the narrator's actual former life, not from a generic office.

3. **Misunderstanding that worsens with every honest sentence.** ARToC: the skulls are versions of himself (ch 313 to 314); strip meant the transformation body (ch 617). Original: the correction must be true and make it worse; stop after the third attempt.

4. **The running gag that mutates.** ARToC: a potato pun as warmth, comfort, probe, dread (ch 616, 637, 643, 642). Original: every running gag gets a scheduled turn to seriousness; a gag that stays a gag is filler.

5. **Comedy mid-chapter, never at the hook.** ARToC: zero comic endings across ch 412 to 470. Original: one comic beat per tense chapter and never inside the turn (voice-guide rule 32); comic endings are for the opening movement and for codas.

6. **The broadest joke directly before the gravest oath.** ARToC: bird mating, then will you risk your lives (ch 736). Original: adjacency is the technique; neither scene reads as filler.

7. **The dim character asks the reader's question.** ARToC: is it like eating two Devil Fruits (ch 700). Original: exposition becomes dialogue (voice-guide rule 29) when the questioner is allowed to be wrong.

8. **Deadpan body horror as etiquette.** ARToC: a head rotated to show a second face, offered as double intuition (ch 473); a bitter pill from a master who asked to be eaten (ch 632). Original: the horror is in the courtesy; do not comment.

## 3. Restraint moves

1. **Objects and counts instead of named grief.** ARToC: porridge for a toothless friend (ch 87); twenty-one bottles on one grave (ch 271); a lighter that will not catch (ch 501). Original: voice-guide rule 21 and 23; one object per beat, and not the first you reached for.

2. **The third party reveals the cost.** ARToC: ch 31; an avatar withholds one sentence and the omission is the payload (ch 300). Original: what the narrator refuses to pass on is the clue (see §6, move 13).

3. **The running gag dropped on the day of loss.** ARToC: thirty-five years of correcting an address; the day the children die he does not, and the text says only that he does not (ch 510). Original: never explain the omission; the reviewer checks that the gag was planted at least three times.

4. **Typographic redaction for the unsayable.** ARToC: ■■ for an erased name (ch 524); ☯ for two refused answers (ch 543). Original: redact rather than withhold; absence made visible lets dialogue continue around it. Use at most twice per book.

5. **Curses as love language, once.** ARToC: a dying woman's farewell is a paragraph of abuse (ch 532); a friend's farewell is a list of grievances ending in an order to come back (ch 620; quoted: "If the words 'this time too' come out of your mouth, I'll rip it apart."). Original: one scene per book; the insult must be specific to the relationship.

6. **Omission by instruction.** ARToC: a face melts while he tells his friend to keep looking forward (ch 265); a goodbye refused, the obituary given as a birthday gift in the last sentence (ch 708). Original: the POV character orders the camera away; the reader supplies what it did not see.

7. **Grief displaced into a conditional or a chore.** ARToC: if he had a body he would probably be weeping (ch 651); a wooden shovel and the word pathetic for his own rambling (ch 330). Original: the dry register at the worst beat (voice-guide rule 23).

8. **Two trees do the goodbye.** ARToC: after a double death, a quince and a lily; the lily's petals do not scatter (ch 325). Original: end a cycle or an arc on an image that grieves for you; no coda (voice-guide rule 18).

## 4. Chapter shape

**Template.** Status line (years elapsed, realm, one concrete change) → one confrontation or lesson → a hinge in the last fifth. Two or three scenes; one may be a cutaway to an antagonist or ally that leaks one fact the hero cannot have. The last line is a name arriving, a sentence that redefines the situation, or the consequence of the turn; it is never a question tacked on (voice-guide rule 38). Open mid-sensation or mid-conversation, not with place (ch 181 to 222 notes: openings like it's cold, it's dark).

**Hook mix targets** (from the book's aggregate of about 830 chapters): hard hooks (cliff or threat) about 40 percent; reveal about 20; quiet about 15; decision about 10; question and comic about 5 each. Tune by movement: the opening ran 25 percent cliff and 28 percent decision; the middle 40 to 55 percent hard; information arcs invert toward reveals. Place a soft close after every very hard one (ch 189 after 188). End one line before the obvious payoff and open the next chapter with it (ch 231 to 232).

**Rest-chapter cadence.** One rest chapter per three to five, never two in a row except as a deliberate reservoir before a cut (ch 93 to 109). Every rest chapter carries one plant that pays within ten chapters and one within sixty (ch 27, 73, 191, 700 to 705).

**Time-compression idioms.** The skip is a one-line paragraph with one concrete anchor the reader can count: years per star (ch 44 to 45); bones crumbled per decade (ch 393); footsteps as millennia (ch 786). Skips sit after a decision and before its test; training is never dramatised (ch 308 to 310). Any span beyond intuition gets a mortal-scale vignette inside it (ch 581 to 583). State the clock once per arc in a single line (ch 703).

**The verdict after the win.** The thematic verdict lands one chapter after the physical victory (ch 692 to 693), or one chapter before it (ch 719 to 720). The fight chapter ends on the fight; the next chapter decides whether it counted.

## 5. First Day / reset template, and how to break it

**The template.** A reset chapter does five jobs in order: (1) re-run a fixed ruler the reader has seen before (a slap and a tree climb, ch 1 to 60) so growth is measured without exposition; (2) audit the ledger (what carried over, what did not, stated as a discovery, never as a list the narrator already knew); (3) state the cycle's goal in one sentence; (4) test one new power on something inert; (5) end on the first changed fact, not on a recap.

**The five ways the book varied it.**

1. **Readings changed, ritual identical** (ch 75, 92, 130): the fox is fled, hunted, forgiven. Use for three consecutive resets at most.
2. **Compressed to a paragraph per beat**, with only the new fact and the new mood expanded (ch 133). Use when the reader has the ritual by heart.
3. **A rule change announced flatly**, one paragraph of consequence, then the plot resumes in the same chapter (ch 140). Use when the anchor moves.
4. **The ritual itself subverted**: the death line spoken and the reset refused (ch 281); the rewind as a chase (ch 326); fifteen minutes (ch 411); a counterfeit the hero audits by smell (ch 457 to 458); the cast staging the ritual themselves (ch 472); the day replaced by an interview with the mechanism's owner (ch 543). Use once each.
5. **The template borrowed for a non-reset**: a realm arrival (ch 139, 339, 356), a dream world (ch 380), a chosen new identity (ch 739), a cycle the narrator cannot remember (ch 789). Rehearse the set piece inside the story so the real subversion needs no re-explanation (ch 380 before 411).

**Rules for when to break the ruler.** Break it only when the break is the plot (the fox refuses the arm, ch 60). Break one step at a time: by ch 609 one safe step of a four-step morning fails, and that is the chapter. Never break the ruler and move the anchor in the same chapter unless the chapter is about the permanence that follows (ch 140). After a break, the next reset is played straight so the reader can hear the difference.

## 6. Mystery moves

These extend `engine/structure/mystery-playbook.md` §3 (camouflage methods) with the book's own repertoire, ranked by how often it appears in the slice tables.

1. **Jargon the narrator cannot parse.** ARToC: an elder speaks of red, blue, and the third; the line is quoted verbatim, misunderstood, and explained nine chapters later when the hero earns the sight (ch 8 to 17). Original: playbook method `misread`; the narrator must quote it exactly so the reader can re-read it.

2. **Plenty-of-nothing.** ARToC: a stele read and dismissed as mundane (ch 51 to 58); a weather eye called worthless twice (ch 66) that saves him (ch 79); stiff shoulders as an idiom (ch 377) that are a passenger (ch 404). Original: playbook method `plenty-of-nothing`; the dismissal must be the narrator's own and stated on the page.

3. **The hero's confident wrong theory, stated as deduction.** ARToC: the fortress exists to build a soul (ch 145, overturned 147); which seat he holds (ch 636, overturned 650). Original: this is the decoy of playbook §4 in a first-person book; the theory must be the most reasonable reading at that stage, and it must die in a scene, not by the narration moving on.

4. **Villain cutaways one secret above the hero.** ARToC: a strike planted in him that he finds a chapter later (ch 147); a mural the reader sees twelve chapters before he does (ch 384 to 396); the knife shown going in before he feels it (ch 494). Original: playbook method `side-pov`; one secret per arc, and the cutaway never leaks anything the main line will not confirm within the arc.

5. **Chapter titles as hidden text.** ARToC: six arc titles are the lines of a mantra (ch 364 to 403). Original: usable once per book; the reader has been trained for hundreds of chapters to read titles as mood.

6. **Cognitive-hazard lore.** ARToC: names that burst heads (ch 185, 189); lore that melts the listener (ch 233); wisdom as salt crystals from the mouth (ch 647). Original: playbook method `cognitive-hazard`; the hazard lets you preserve a mystery without cheating, because the character cannot pursue what the reader keeps.

7. **Refusal as information.** ARToC: a source refuses to answer a grouped set of questions, and the silhouette of the refusals is the answer (ch 726); a villain names the two topics he forbids (ch 506). Original: list the refused questions together on the page; the reader will dig where you pointed.

8. **Let the antagonist win the argument.** ARToC: heart versus fate conceded; the hero wins by refusing the premise (ch 734); villains' lectures stated to be correct (ch 638). Original: a coherent pedagogy is the camouflage for a coherent plan; refuting it must cost the hero a philosophy of his own.

9. **The fake-out death via POV withholding.** ARToC: a hostage dies for a whole chapter because the narrator never returns to "I" until the last paragraph (ch 512); three slice-of-life chapters are a dream inside a salt pillar (ch 275 to 277). Original: the fake-out holds only if every sentence was true in-world (playbook §1, rule 2); keep the first-person narrator off the page for the whole sequence.

10. **The mannerism that is the secret.** ARToC: a shoulder pat presented as fake camaraderie, repeated five times, revealed as a memory probe (ch 181 to 218); a laugh used for five hundred chapters given an origin (ch 506 to 525). Original: playbook method `tic`; the narrator must dismiss the tic on the page so the reader stops watching it; cash it as mechanism, not as character.

11. **The kept object.** ARToC: a pendant tracked across nine cycles (ch 99 to 440); a withered hand kissed and set down so its return is a shock (ch 687 to 692). Original: the Foreknowledge Ledger's carry-over item; let the character put it down before it matters.

12. **The narrator bookmarks a contradiction he cannot solve.** ARToC: a logic gap in a ritual noted and left (ch 626), paid exactly (ch 646). Original: naming the gap makes the answer feel owed; schedule the payoff when you write the bookmark.

13. **Plant the key as something the narrator refuses to pass on.** ARToC: an avatar withholds one sentence of hers (ch 300), reprised (ch 310), paid at his death (ch 325). Original: playbook §1 rule 6 (theories may be withheld, facts may not) is satisfied because the reader sees that something was withheld.

14. **Children's books and diaries carry the cosmology.** ARToC: a flat world in a fairy tale (ch 101 to 103); an astronomer's shaking handwriting (ch 117); a storybook with seven chapters where there should be eight (ch 331). Original: playbook method `exposition` with the dread turned up; the hero should call it nonsense on the page.

15. **Typographic corruption as a clue.** ARToC: a sentence runs together at the glimpse of something vast (ch 139, 169, 177), so it reads as a typo and is not interrogated; a glyph marks tampered history without explanation (ch 615, 642). Original: last resort, for the unrenderable only; it works because the surrounding prose is plain.

## 7. System and cost moves

1. **One breakthrough per cycle or arc, at the point of death or loss.** ARToC: peak (ch 16), Three Flowers (ch 24), the Five Energies (ch 34), all at death; later one per arc, each followed by proof the tier is insufficient (ch 631, 644, 650). Original: schedule breakthroughs in the arc plan with their cost named first.

2. **Costs sensory, never numeric.** ARToC: fingerprints worn off (ch 44); a bitter pill (ch 632); a trampled potato flower (ch 642). Original: if the cost is a number, the reader skims it.

3. **Humiliation before gain.** ARToC: shattered by protective qi alone (ch 48) before rescue; whipped as a potato-peeler (ch 462 to 463) before an Immortal Art; 400,000 years as a petal (ch 498). Original: a realm that arrives without loss reads as fiat.

4. **Pay for every power with a named person.** ARToC: six divine powers, six disciples' funerals (ch 430 to 440). Original: the reader learns the system by counting graves.

5. **Hard ceilings alternating with power-stripping.** ARToC: a Flag caps lower-realm output at a mortal tier while the true body is a god (ch 547, 587); the whole cast reduced to mortals for a century (ch 707 to 717); then an enemy class above the stated cap (ch 725). Original: raise the ceiling and lower the floor in the same arc so fights stay legible.

6. **Lore in one lecture per concept, then used.** ARToC: realm stages explained once (ch 192, 206, 213) and never re-explained; a system lecture mid-battle justifies why a seventh strike exists two pages later (ch 324). Original: a lecture that does not change what a character can do within two pages is cut.

7. **The externalised save file, later made the problem.** ARToC: a manual the friend improves each life (ch 5 to 47); the memory ledger as the infection that trained the villain (ch 529); the ledger stolen so the hero cannot recall a place name (ch 307 to 316). Original: whatever persists across resets is also what can be taken.

8. **Milestone, not calendar.** ARToC: years per star (ch 44 to 45); realm names as thesis statements (ch 403, 790). Original: a time skip is legitimate only when its result is dramatised (craft-report §5.3, lesson 5).

## 8. What not to imitate

- **Translation artifacts.** Romanised sound effects; capitalised compound technique names without articles; tense wobble inside paragraphs; honorifics left in; translator notes inside prose; interleaved divine speech that needs a footnote (ch 262); pronoun flips for ungendered beings; watermark strings. None of it is the author's prose.
- **Over-explanation.** Whole-chapter realm lectures (ch 41, 136, 545); a literal table (ch 564); battle chapters 70 to 80 percent blow-by-blow with the reveal in the last tenth (ch 70 to 72, 121 to 127); mechanics explained by the villain when needed (ch 457). Keep the last tenth.
- **Melodrama.** The author apologises for it (ch 453) and for over-length (ch 583). One unframed cry per book (ch 602), scheduled.
- **Reset-erases-relationships.** The lover who does not know him (ch 137, 363) was the readers' chief structural complaint. Decide the carry-over before chapter one.
- **Comedy quarantined in side stories.** A content warning precedes them (ch 813) and readers called the split a flaw. Keep comic relief inside the main line.
- **Scale inflation.** Trillions in a sentence (ch 418, 426); loop arithmetic a translator had to correct (ch 682). Count deaths, breaths, or footsteps, not years.
- **Long cycles without a First Day.** The 59-, 45-, and 85-chapter cycles are where readers quit (craft-report §5.3). Cap near forty; put a realm arrival where a reset would have been.
- **Self-aware mockery of your own system without a reason.** A villain reviewing the flagship technique (ch 462; quoted: "Maybe you can crush a sandcastle. Change the name.") works once because humiliation precedes a breakthrough; as a tic it undermines the book.

## 9. Lint calibration note

The corpus baseline (`engine/style/baselines/regression-xianxia-webnovel.json`, 845 files) reports: mean sentence 12.1 words, coefficient of variation 0.74, mean paragraph 14 words, dialogue share 21 percent, punchline share 36 percent, ellipses 15.5 per thousand words, exclamation marks 12 per thousand, -ly adverbs 20.7 per thousand, rhetorical questions 3.0 per thousand, filter verbs 8.5 per thousand, thought verbs 6.2 per thousand, em-dashes 1.5 per thousand, tricolons 0.4 per thousand.

**Keep (these are the mode):**
- Short mean sentence with high variation (12 words, CV about 0.74): a column of three-word lines broken by one 30-word exposition sentence. Voice-guide rule 6 is satisfied by the exposition paragraph.
- Short paragraphs (mean 14 words; one sentence as default): the declared inversion of rule 15, stated in the bible.
- Dialogue share near 20 percent: interiority is calculation and bare statement; dialogue arrives as argument, mistake, or refusal (rule 29).
- Thought verbs at about six per thousand: exactly the house budget (rule 19).
- Rhetorical questions at about three per thousand: the cap in rule 25, with the bible's declaration.
- Low tricolon rate (0.4 per thousand): repetition is anaphora and the same line thrice, not three-item lists (rule 14).

**Ignore as translation noise (do not target them):**
- Ellipses at 15 per thousand and exclamation marks at 12 per thousand: Korean web-fiction typography carried through translation. An original English book sits near the house default; the lint must not reward matching the baseline.
- -ly adverbs at 21 per thousand: an adverb where Korean uses a verb ending. Hold the house target under 12 (rule 9).
- Filter verbs at 8.5 per thousand: partly translation; rule 20 rations them.
- Em-dashes at 1.5 per thousand: the book's em-dash-led lines for transmitted speech. The house bans em-dashes (rule 10); mark transmitted speech by a fence the bible defines and keep the count at zero.

**Flag for the reviewer:**
- Punchline share at 36 percent is above rule 16. In this mode most paragraphs are one sentence, so the metric measures the few multi-sentence ones; those end on a mundane detail or an unanswered line. Target under 30 percent.
- Lines that look like typographic damage (run-together words, redaction glyphs, a degrading name) must be logged as deliberate (ch 139, 524, 722) or a copy pass will fix them.

One line to remember the whole mode: a friend's instruction that the hero spends 250 chapters misreading, "Eun-hyun, swing. It's already in your hand." (ch 79). The move is already in the ledger; the chapter's job is to make the reader feel the narrator arrive at it.
