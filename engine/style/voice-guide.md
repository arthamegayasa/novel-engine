# Voice guide: the house style

This is the default prose stance for every project in the engine. A project's bible §2 narrows it (a particular narrator, a particular register); it never loosens it. The guide is written as rules because the reviewer has to be able to point at a line and say which rule it breaks.

Evidence behind the rules is in `research/craft-report.md` §1 and §2. Short version: what makes machine prose hollow is not any single word but a cluster of habits (significance inflation, explaining what was just shown, balanced clauses, tidy triplets, uniform cadence, frictionless dialogue). Removing the habits is the goal. Fooling a detector is not.

## 1. Stance

1. **The narrator is a person, not a camera and not an essayist.** They have a vocabulary ceiling, blind spots, favourite comparisons, and things they refuse to look at. Narration may be wrong. The text later shows it was wrong.
2. **Scene before summary.** Default to dramatised action in a specific place at a specific hour. Summary is allowed for transitions and time passage and must carry one concrete detail that could not be guessed.
3. **Enter late, leave early.** Cut arrivals, greetings, goodbyes, and walking to places unless the walk is the scene.
4. **The reader is smarter than the narrator thinks.** Never explain a gesture, a joke, an implication, or a clue. If a line needs the explanation to land, fix the line.
5. **Omission is only allowed when the ledger knows the omitted fact** (Hemingway's iceberg; `clues.md` and `continuity.md` are the submerged seven-eighths).

## 2. Sentences

6. **Vary length on purpose.** In any 1,000 words: at least one sentence over 40 words with a real syntactic shape (not a comma chain), and several under five words. Fragments are legal. A sentence of one word is legal once per chapter.
7. **No two consecutive sentences of the same shape.** Shape means: subject-verb-object; participial opener; subordinate-clause opener; fragment; question; list. The lint flags runs of near-identical length; the reviewer reads for shape.
8. **Concrete subjects, active verbs.** "The kettle screamed" beats "there was a screaming from the kettle." Weak verbs (is, has, seems, feels, gets) are allowed when the plainness is the point.
9. **Adverbs earn their place.** Target under 12 "-ly" adverbs per 1,000 words in narration. Replace with a better verb or cut.
10. **Em-dashes: none.** Use a comma, a colon, a full stop, or parentheses once a chapter at most. (The ban is a house choice; the research shows the dash is a weak tell by itself, but the dash habit correlates with the "aside-within-aside" rhythm we want gone.)
11. **Semicolons: rare,** and only in a voice that would use them.
12. **No trailing explainer clauses.** A sentence that ends ", a gesture that revealed her fear" or ", underscoring his resolve" ends at the gesture.
13. **No negative-parallel scaffolds as a habit.** "Not X but Y," "it wasn't X, it was Y," "no X, no Y, just Z": at most one per chapter, and only when the contrast is the point of the scene.
14. **Triplets are suspicious.** Default to one or two items. A third item is allowed if it breaks the pattern of the first two.

## 3. Paragraphs

15. **Paragraphs are breaths, not boxes.** Range from one line to ten within a scene. The one-line paragraph is a hammer: once per chapter, at the turn.
16. **Not every paragraph ends on a punch.** Target: fewer than a third of multi-sentence paragraphs end on a short zinger. Let paragraphs end on a mundane detail, a half-thought, or a line of dialogue that goes unanswered.
17. **No thesis openers.** Do not begin a paragraph with a conclusion the paragraph then illustrates. Start with the first concrete detail.
18. **No coda.** The chapter does not end with a reflective paragraph that tells the reader what the chapter meant. It ends on the consequence of the turn or on an image.

## 4. Interiority and emotion

19. **Thought verbs are rationed** (Palahniuk): knew, realised, understood, felt, wanted, remembered, wondered, believed. Budget: six per 1,000 words in narration. Unpack the rest into perception and action.
20. **Filter words are rationed:** saw, heard, noticed, watched, seemed, appeared. The point of view is already established; render the thing.
21. **Emotions are not named unless the naming is wrong or ironic.** Render through: the object the character fixates on, what they do with their hands, what they fail to answer, what they count, what they tidy. One physical reaction per beat, and not the first one you reached for (no clenched jaws, no breaths they did not know they were holding).
22. **Reaction order** (Swain's motivation-reaction unit): stimulus, then involuntary reaction, then deliberate action, then speech. Speech never arrives before the body.
23. **Pull back when it hurts** (Maass). At the most painful beat, go dry, go practical, go to an inventory of objects. The reader supplies the grief.
24. **Interiority contradicts action at least once per chapter**, and the narrator does not reconcile the two.
25. **Rhetorical questions in narration** are allowed only in a voice that bible §2 says uses them, and then at most three per 1,000 words. "Was this what he wanted?" is usually a declarative in hiding.
26. **Psychic distance moves one step at a time** (Gardner). Open scenes at middle distance; descend into close sensory fragments only at the turn; climb back out before the chapter ends or stay close on purpose.

## 5. Dialogue

27. **Idiolect is mandatory.** Each named speaker has, in the bible: typical sentence length, two favourite words, two words they never use, a comparison domain, and how they lie. Cover the tags and the speaker must still be identifiable.
28. **People do not answer the question asked.** At least one exchange per dialogue scene where a speaker deflects, lies, misunderstands, or answers a different question.
29. **No information trades.** Nobody says what both speakers already know. Exposition arrives as argument, as mistake, or as a thing someone refuses to say.
30. **Subtext quota:** at least one line per dialogue scene means the opposite of its surface.
31. **Action beats, not adverbs,** carry tone. "He said, coldly" becomes what he does with the cup.
32. **Humour comes from character and register clash,** not from the narrator winking. A salaryman's vocabulary against an immortal's; a precise person in a chaotic room. One comic beat per tense chapter, never inside the turn itself.

## 6. Description and world

33. **One unexpected specific beats three generic adjectives.** "A smell of pine and birdsong" is a list; "the resin had glued a moth to the gatepost" is a detail.
34. **Numbers are exact when the character would know them.** Prices, distances, years, counts. The narrator who counts is a narrator with a mind.
35. **The world is used, not toured.** Objects have wear, owners, and prices. No worldbuilding paragraph that is not also doing character or plot work.
36. **One figurative comparison per paragraph at most.** Draw comparisons from the narrator's own experience (an office worker compares to spreadsheets and overtime, not to things he has never seen).
37. **Sensory detail is chosen by the character's need,** not by a checklist of senses.

## 7. Chapter level

38. **One scene goal, one turn, one hook.** The turn lands in the last 15 percent. The hook is the consequence of the turn, not a new question tacked on.
39. **Open in motion.** No recap of the previous chapter. If the reader needs a fact from before, a character needs it now.
40. **Each chapter has one crowded passage** (Le Guin: dense specific detail, the scene slowed to its grain) **and one leap** (a transition or time skip done in a clause).
41. **A comic or banal beat inside a tense scene, and a tense beat inside a quiet one.** Monotone sentiment is a tell.
42. **Revision is subtractive.** The line pass cuts eight to twelve percent. It does not reword into synonyms; that produces elegant variation, another tell.

## 8. What "human" looks like in practice

- A digression that pays off sideways two chapters later.
- A narrator who gets a minor fact wrong and is corrected by someone.
- A joke that falls flat in-world and the silence afterward.
- A physical inconvenience during an important conversation.
- A sentence that stops before the point because the narrator cannot finish the thought.
- A list where one item does not belong, and nobody comments.
- A wrong guess, stated with confidence.
- Repetition used as rhythm: the same short sentence three times across a chapter, meaning something different each time.

## 9. Tests the reviewer runs

- **Read-aloud test:** three paragraphs; does the cadence vary?
- **Cover test:** cover dialogue tags; can you name the speaker?
- **Label test:** find every named emotion; could the scene stand without it?
- **Explainer test:** find every sentence that explains the one before; cut it; does the paragraph still work?
- **Find-the-clue test:** given a clue ID, can you locate it without being told where? Too fast means the camouflage failed; impossible means the plant is missing.
