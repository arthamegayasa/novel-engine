# Chapter 1: Clock

The mud had ice in it. Not the fur the blood-bank fridge grows when somebody leaves the door: ice through it, in the ruts. I put my right hand down to push myself up. The skin broke and the water underneath was colder than the ice.

The hand had been closed. There was nothing in it. I opened it to push and then could not have said what I had been holding, and I put that aside, because there was a sound.

Wall suction. That was the nearest thing. An outlet left running with no catheter on the end of it, the hiss with the roar inside, except that it came from the whole of the air in front of me. The air there was open. A slit about the height of a door, standing on nothing, the edges turned inward, and grey through it, and grey around it.

The trees on either side were pines and they leaned away from it, all of them, at the same angle, the way the plastic chairs in the ER corridor lean away from the drunk. Above the pines was a ceiling. Grey, solid, poured and left, about as far up as the roof of the hospital, eight floors. The pines were taller than that and bent to fit under it.

Six swords were planted point-down in a row in the mud, a stride apart, and at each sword a person was kneeling with one hand on the hilt, as if they had been placed. Pak Darto from the ground-floor door, in the security shirt with the epaulettes. Mbak Rini from the ER, in the maroon they wear downstairs. A man in the lead-apron green of radiology. The cook from the two o'clock cart, apron on. An ambulance driver in the yellow vest. A man in a batik shirt and bathroom slippers, which is what you wear when you have been sleeping on the chair beside someone's bed for a week. Six. And me, past the end of the row, on the mud, with no sword.

Rini was the first to say anything that was a word. "IGD Tjipta Mandala, selamat malam." She said it into her hand, which was empty, the way she says it into the phone. She looked at the hand and then along the row and then at me.

"Dok. Dok, you're, it's on your badge. Wulan. Neuro, fourth floor. I know you from the lifts." She was on her knees still and had not taken her other hand off the hilt. "We're from. This is. Dok, this is the hospital, these are all, Pak Darto's from the front, and that's the cart, and. Nobody from four. You're going to ask and I don't know, I was on the phone. The ceiling came in over the triage desk and I was on the phone. Dok, you've still got your clogs on."

I did not look down. Three, five, seven, eight, ten, and the other six, in bed order. I counted twice.

"Eleven beds on four," I said. "Anybody in a gown? A trolley? Rini. Look past me."

"There's trees past you, Dok."

"Somebody could have gone round them."

"Then ask Pak Darto. He was facing that way. I was looking at my hand."

"The fourth floor came down at two forty-eight."

"You said."

Eleven beds and one doctor awake. Six people with swords. Me on the mud.

The men came out of the pines. Grey coats, swords on the hip, a handcart with blankets. Behind them a fire had burned down into deep ash. They had been expecting a delivery. Four went to the row and one came to me.

He walked past the swords and past the six and past his own men without turning his head, the way the men on the new-wing site used to walk it, eyes on the ground for what might give. A frontal sort of man. Nothing in the face that was not needed for the job. He stopped a stride off and said something to me and it had the shape of a sentence and I got none of it, so I asked him the time. Time since the event is the first number you take.

"Jam berapa?" I said, and he looked at me for longer than the question needed, and then he spoke over his shoulder, and an old man came up from the fire with a book under his arm and the smell of a pipe on him.

The old man knelt in the mud without minding it. He had a thumb that was black to the first joint with ink, and he took my jaw in his other hand the way you take a jaw to test the gag, and put the black thumb on my tongue. Ink and pipe. He looked at me while he held it there, and the look went on past the point where a look is for anything, and I let him, because my hands were under me in the water and because he had the book.

Then he took the thumb out and said something to the man standing over me, and the man standing over me answered, and the answer began as noise and ended as words.

"On my right," he was saying, "and keep talking; the tear's loud."

"The tongue takes," the old man said, to him, getting up.

Two of the grey coats had come up behind him with a blanket. He said it again to me, the same words, as if I had been slow. It is what you do in the ambulance with a head. You keep them talking so that you know when they stop.

"It's two forty-eight," I said.

"Keep talking."

"There's a discharge note on bed three. Half written. Pak Yunus goes home Tuesday if his swallow holds. He'll ask if it's subuh. Tell him belum. The alarm on seven is the lead again, somebody needs to tape it this time instead of pressing it back. Bed five's on day three of burst suppression and the levetiracetam's due at four. The cart was late. Bed eight was."

"Keep talking."

My teeth had started by then, and talking through them is a thing you can do for about a sentence. I counted instead, which is talking, and which he allowed.

He counted too. Under his breath, before he bent, in his own language, which I now had: one to eight, the way you would count before lifting a patient. When he had got to eight he lifted me, one arm under the knees and one under the back, and because the two men had put the blanket round me while I was being counted over it came with me to the fire, where he set me down on his right and stayed, with the fire in front of us and the row of swords between us and the tear.

Along the row the old man did to the six what he had done to me, the jaw and the thumb and the look. Round the thumb the cook tried to say something and was held until he stopped.

Then the four at the row went along it, one word to each, not a word for me. At the word, each of the six drew the sword up out of the ground. Darto first, with both hands, the way he lifts the barrier arm when the motor fails. Rini one-handed, and then both. The radiographer. The cook, whose first act with a sword in his hand was to feel his apron pocket for his glasses. His pocket had come and his glasses had not. Nobody had come with anything they were not wearing. The driver, who had said nothing until then, swore, and his sword came out on the swear. Last, the man in the batik shirt, who had to be told twice, and who said a woman's name to the man telling him, as a question, and was not answered.

Frost on the blades. In their hands it went to water. All six. I counted it the way you count a row of pupils, left to right, and then right to left, and the water ran off the steel and down their wrists and none of them dropped the thing.

Nothing in my hand.

The man beside me said one word, and one of his men came round the fire with his own sword held out hilt first.

"Grip, not the edge," the man said.

I took it. It was colder than anything on the ward including the fridge, and the frost on it stayed frost, and went up the hilt into my palm, and when I gave it back the palm came off the steel with a sound, and left a shape.

"Six set," the man beside me said, to his men, "six took," and then, to me, "Noted."

They brought the six round the fire with their swords held out like something that might spill. Six set. Seven came. I had landed past the end of the row; I was the one nobody had set a sword for. I warmed the hand on the blanket where the steel had been.

The light stayed the grey it had been and the fire got brighter against it.

"It looks like evening," I said to one of the grey coats feeding the fire.

"Past the noon sag," he said. "It always looks like evening here, the sky's that low."

"How low?" I said, and he looked up at it the way you look at a ceiling you have stopped seeing.

"Here? Low."

"In floors. Storeys."

"It's low," he said, and put another branch on, and I let it be, and filed the noon.

Once the six were round the fire I went along them: name, breathing, colour, both hands. The man who had carried me came too, and stood on my left. All six had both hands. All six were breathing. The cook's colour was bad and his hands were worse, and I took the sword off his knees and put it on the ground beside him and nobody stopped me.

"Pak, your name."

"Sugeng, Dok."

"What time did you come up?"

"The cart goes up at two," he said, and looked at the sword on the ground as if it might go up as well. "I was on the stairs, the lift's slow at that hour. Dok, is it always this cold?"

"Pak. Both hands."

"I asked if it's always cold."

"I got here when you did."

"They carried you. I thought they'd told you something."

"They told me to keep talking. Open the other hand."

The radiographer had both hands out before I asked. "I was in the basement, Dok," he said, as if it were a confession. "CT's in the basement. Nobody comes down at that hour, nobody from four, I'd have seen the trolley if anyone from four had come down."

"Did I ask?"

"No, Dok."

The driver had his hands under his arms. "Don't," he said, before I got to him. "I'm fine. I was in the bay with the engine running, I'm fine, do the others." I did the others and came back to him last.

Rini had both hands round her hilt and the blade pointed away from her, the way she holds a sharps bin. "Dok," she said, when I got to her, "are we dead?"

"Two forty-eight," I said. "The fourth floor came down at two forty-eight."

"That's not an answer, Dok."

"Both hands. Open them." She opened them, and they were fine, and I went on to the next.

The man in the batik shirt gave me the woman's name when I asked for his. "Sari," he said. "She's in Obgyn. Second floor."

"Your name, Pak."

"You can write hers first. I can tell you the bed."

"I haven't got anything to write on."

"That old man has. Ask him. Ask if he has her down already."

I went on to Darto. The man watched me go.

Pak Darto had gone and sat where he could see the whole of the fire and both ends of the row, which is where he sits in the lobby, with his sword across his knees and his hands not on it.

"Bu Dokter," he said, "saya jaga di sini."

"Pak, you don't have to."

"Anyone from four, Pak? Behind the trees?"

"Nobody passed me."

"You were looking?"

"I look, Bu Dokter. It's what they pay me for."

"Saya jaga di sini." He looked past me at the tear, and then at the grey coats, and then at the tear, which is how he looks at the car park, and I left him to it.

"Read-back," the old man with the book said, "so that it is as you said it," and went round the six with the book open. I took this for procedure, like reading back a verbal order, and I was right about that at least.

"You said, and I quote," he said to Rini, and read it flat, all of it, the name of the hospital with the consonants in the wrong places.

"Yes. That's the phone. That's what I say on the phone."

"I have it as you said it."

The driver's was the swear. The old man read it with the same care, and the grey coats round the fire liked that, and said it back to each other.

"Can it be something else?"

"It is what you said."

"I say a lot of things."

"That was the first."

Nobody from the hospital laughed. The man in the batik shirt's was the woman's name, and the old man read it to him exactly.

"Yes," the man said. "Where is she?"

The old man turned the page, and then it was me.

"You said, and I quote," he said, and read it, "jam brapa," giving it two syllables and a half, "and what is it."

"What time is it," I said.

There was a pause round the fire of the kind there is on a ward round when the student has answered a question nobody asked, and then one of the grey coats said a word, and the others took it up, and the man beside me said it too.

"Clock," he said.

The old man wrote. I watched the page. I could not read a letter of it. He had six names in a column under a heading. He drew a line under the sixth with the edge of his thumb and the pen, and wrote mine below the line.

"What does the heading say?"

"A clerical matter," the man beside me said, before the old man could, and the old man closed the book.

"The Post's a day's span," the man beside me said, "and we walk at first light."

"What's a span?"

"What's held." He looked at the sky, and then at the row of empty holes where the swords had stood. "A sky over a Post is held or it isn't. Ours is a day's walk of it, and the yard's under the lowest of it, which is why the Post is where it is."

"Held how?"

"Braced. You'll see the yard, and the braces, and what they're under."

"How high is it there?"

"Lower."

"Lower than eight floors? That's what I'm asking."

"Bring me a floor and I'll measure it. Can you walk?"

"I can walk. Can they?"

"They'll have to. We leave at first light."

The grey coats were loading the cart. "Eightbreath wants it done before the dark," one of them said across the fire to another, and jerked his head at the man on my left.

I placed the name. A stance name, the sort of thing a man gives himself when he has decided on a number and kept to it. The physio on six has her people count before a transfer, and theirs is three.

"Give Clock the other blanket," somebody said, and somebody did, and I did not look up.

"You'll walk," the man on my left said. "The cart's for the swords."

"I can walk."

The six slept with their swords, each with a hand on the hilt, as if they had been placed. Mine was the blanket, and the hand that had held the steel, which had come back as far as the second joint, and the time, which was two forty-eight and would be two forty-eight until somebody here gave me a better number. Rini had read my name off my chest, and it was not a question about the hour.

"Clock," somebody said across the fire, to somebody else, and I looked up anyway.
